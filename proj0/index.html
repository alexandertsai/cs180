Project 0: Becoming Friends with Your Camera

CS180 — Intro to Computer Vision and Computational Photography, Fall 2026

The goal of this project is to build some intuition for perspective, focal length, and the center of projection. All three parts use the same idea: if you change where the camera stands and change the focal length to keep the subject the same size in the frame, the subject looks roughly the same but everything around it changes dramatically. Distance to the subject — not the lens — is what controls perspective.

Part 1: Selfie — The Wrong Way and The Right Way

Two portraits of the same face, framed to about the same size. The first was shot up close at a 24 mm equivalent focal length. The second was shot from several feet back at 227 mm.

Close, no zoom — 24 mm
Camera roughly an arm's length away.
Far, zoomed in — 227 mm
Several feet back, zoomed to match the framing.

Why the second one looks better. In the close shot the camera is only a foot or so from the face, so the nose is meaningfully closer to the lens than the ears are — maybe 20–30% closer. Because objects shrink with distance, that difference gets recorded: the nose and the cheeks balloon outward, the ears fall away, and the head takes on a rounded, stretched look. This is the standard "selfie face."

Stepping back to several feet makes the nose-to-ear distance a tiny fraction of the total camera-to-face distance, so every part of the face is at essentially the same depth and gets scaled by the same amount. The result is a flatter, more even, more familiar-looking face. Zooming in only crops and magnifies — it does not change perspective. The improvement comes entirely from moving the camera back.

Part 2: Architectural Perspective

The facade of the Life Sciences Building, shot twice at roughly the same size in frame: once from far away at 53 mm, and once from up close at 19 mm.

Far, zoomed in — 53 mm
Compressed and flattened.
Close, no zoom — 19 mm
Exaggerated depth.

What changed. In the zoomed shot the building reads almost like a flat elevation drawing. The columns stay close to vertical and parallel, the row of columns across the facade is nearly uniform in width, and the steps in the foreground are shallow and unremarkable. Because the camera is far away, the near and far edges of the building differ only slightly in distance, so they are drawn at nearly the same scale and the depth of the structure collapses.

Walking up to the building and going wide inverts all of that. The stairs, which are now only a few feet from the lens, dominate the bottom of the frame and rush away from the viewer. The columns splay outward and lean, and lines that are parallel in the real building visibly converge. The facade now reads as a three-dimensional object with real depth instead of a flat surface. Again, the lens change only kept the building filling the frame; the perspective change came from standing closer.

Part 3: Dolly Zoom (the "Vertigo Shot")

Ten frames of a stone fountain, shot while walking backwards and zooming in at the same time so the fountain stayed about the same size in every frame. The focal length climbs from 24 mm in the first frame to 184 mm in the last. The GIF below plays the sequence forward and back.

Subject fixed, background breathing in and out.

What is happening. The fountain is locked in place, but the wall, the brick, and the plants behind it swell forward and then fall back. Walking backwards shrinks everything in the scene, but it shrinks the nearby fountain much faster than the far wall, since the same few feet of retreat is a large fractional change for the fountain and a small one for the background. Zooming in to restore the fountain's size then magnifies the background along with it, so the background ends up much larger relative to the subject than it started. The scene appears to compress and flatten around a subject that never moves.

Takeaway

The same lesson shows up three times. Perspective — how strongly near things loom over far things — is set by where the center of projection sits relative to the scene, and by nothing else. Focal length only decides how much of the resulting projection you crop into the frame. That is why the fix for a bad portrait is to back up, why a building looks flat from across the street, and why the dolly zoom works at all: it changes the two knobs in opposite directions so one subject stays put while the world behind it warps.

All photographs taken with an iPhone. Focal lengths are 35 mm equivalents read from image EXIF data.
