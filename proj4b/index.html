A.2: Recover Homographies

The homography is a powerful tool to transform images. We define it as a 3 × 3 matrix that maps homogeneous image coordinates between views. The nine entries have eight degrees of freedom: multiplying every entry by the same nonzero constant scales all three output coordinates equally, and dividing by the third coordinate cancels that constant. We fix the bottom-right entry to 1 to remove this scale ambiguity. The extra terms g and h allow perspective effects, meaning that parallel lines need not remain parallel (unlike affine transformations). For this matrix, c and f provide translation terms before division.

To understand how we solve our system of equations, we first observe that each matching pair (x, y) → (u, v) contributes two equations. We multiply by the common denominator, collect the coefficients of a to h, and organise the equations into matrices. A contains those known coefficients, the vector h contains our eight unknown entries of H, and b contains the corresponding destination coordinates u and v.

u=ax+by+cgx+hy+1v=dx+ey+fgx+hy+1
[xy1000−xu−yu000xy1−xv−yv][abcdefgh]=[uv]


Ah=b

Four point pairs provide the minimum eight equations, but a fit based on only four pairs is sensitive to small coordinate errors. I use additional pairs and solve the overdetermined system with least squares, minimizing the sum of squared residuals in the linear equations. Before solving, I center and scale both point sets so their mean distance from the origin is √2. This improves numerical conditioning. I then transform the solution back to the original image coordinates.

A.3: Warp and Rectify Images

Here, we use inverse warping. For each destination pixel, we apply an inverse homography, divide by the resulting third coordinate, and sample the source image. This mitigates the issue of holes: forward mapping can cause source pixels to land between the aforementioned destination pixels and leave holes. If a destination pixel maps outside the source image, I simply mark that destination pixel invalid. To determine the output canvas, we transform the four source corners with H, taking the minimum and maximum x and y coordinates to determine the top left and top right and bottom left and bottom right limits. If the bounds include negative coordinates, I translate the canvas just enough for everything to be non-negative.

Next, after knowing where the pixels are, we need to know what colors they should be! The homography gives us a source location (x, y), which may fall between pixels. Let I(x, y) denote a source pixel’s color. We can define the upper-left integer neighbor and the fractional distances from it (how far our mapped source location is from that upper-left neighbor) as:

x0=⌊x⌋y0=⌊y⌋dx=x−x0dy=y−y0

Nearest-neighbor sampling rounds the source coordinate to the nearest integer pixel and copies its color:

INN(x,y)=I(⌊x+0.5⌋,⌊y+0.5⌋)

Bilinear sampling instead combines the four neighboring colors. We weight them based on how close they are to the source location (closer neighbors receive greater weights):

IBL(x,y)=(1−dx)(1−dy)I(x0,y0)+dx(1−dy)I(x0+1,y0)+(1−dx)dyI(x0,y0+1)+dxdyI(x0+1,y0+1)

The target aspect ratios for rectification were estimated, not measured. The four weights sum to one. Coordinates outside the source bounds are marked invalid. For valid coordinates at the right or bottom boundary, neighbor indices are clamped to the last available pixel; tiny floating-point boundary errors are also clamped.

A.4: Blend Images into Mosaics

For each set, I keep the center photograph as the reference and estimate a homography from the left and right images onto that reference. Transforming all source corners (first apply H to corners and find bounds, then subtract by appropriate amounts to determine the canvas) gives the bounds of a shared canvas, including its offset from the reference origin. I inverse-warp each image into this canvas and utilise a binary mask to check validity. This mask identifies which canvas pixels have valid source data.

To blend the images together, we use feathering: pixels farther from each warped image’s valid boundary receive larger weights. I compute a weighted average at every covered pixel, dividing by the sum of weights. This normalization avoids increasing brightness simply because several images overlap. Pixels with no contributing image remain transparent in the exported mosaic.

B.2: Patch descriptors

We first use Harris to detect corners in each image. We then use ANMS to select up to 800 corners per image. For each selected corner, we sample a 40×40 neighborhood from the blurred grayscale image, taking samples five pixels apart horizontally and vertically. This produces an 8×8 patch. We normalize the patch by subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation, reducing sensitivity to brightness and contrast fluctuations. We represent the resulting patch as a list of 64 values called a descriptor. We compare descriptors across images and look for similar values, which suggest that two corners correspond to the same physical point in the scene.

We compare descriptors using Euclidean distance: distance = np.sqrt(np.sum((descriptor_a - descriptor_b) ** 2)). For each descriptor, we find its closest and second-closest matches in the other image. We keep the closest match if its distance divided by the second-closest distance is less than 0.75.

We also require mutual matching. If corner A’s closest match is corner B, then corner B’s closest match must also be corner A.

If we scrutinize where some of the blurring occurred in the first image, you might be able to see that a couple of the lines near the chair don't perfectly align with their designated corner. Focusing specifically on the brown triangular chair at the bottom, it is also possible that the corners detected within that chair have repetitive surrounding textures, which could cause mismatches.

B.4: RANSAC and mosaics

We randomly select four candidate matching pairs. Each pair contains one point from the source image and the proposed corresponding point in the other image. We then compute a homography. After computing this homography, we apply it to transform the source point in every candidate pair and measure its distance from its proposed match. Pairs with an error below three pixels are called inliers. We repeat this process for 4,000 random groups of four, calculating the number of inliers for each group. We keep the homography with the most inliers and recompute it using all of those inliers.
